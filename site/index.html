When patients leave the hospital, does better discharge information go with fewer returns?

A discharge is the transition from a hospital stay to home or another care setting. This study asks whether hospitals’ reported discharge information is associated with unplanned readmission within 30 days.

01 / What we can observe

Most hospitals report
high discharge scores.

Across the 64 acute-care hospitals, published discharge-information scores range from 81% to 94%, with a median of 89%. These describe what survey respondents reported, after CMS adjustment for patient mix and survey mode.

The composite combines discussion of help after discharge and written information about symptoms or problems to watch for. A high score does not establish that patients understood the plan or that a particular handout worked.

Source: CMS HCAHPS, H_COMP_6_Y_P. October 1, 2024–September 30, 2025. Hospital estimates are rounded as published. Each hospital counts once; this is not a patient-weighted Wisconsin average.

02 / Why the comparison stops here

A shared hospital ID
is only the first step.

The outcome is unavailable.

All 64 acute-care hospitals’ Hybrid_HWR scores, denominators and interval bounds are marked Not Available. Each carries CMS footnote 4: suppression for one or more quarters. Missing rates are not zero rates, and suppression does not tell us that a hospital performed poorly.

The older claims-based hospital-wide measure ID is absent. Condition-specific readmission measures answer different questions and were not substituted.

The time windows also differ.

Both files cover a year, but the same year is not being measured. They overlap for 273 days; none of the 64 has identical periods. Even with available outcomes, this release would fail the protocol’s date rule.

A useful negative feasibility result

We have not measured whether discharge information predicts readmission. The data needed for that comparison are missing. This is not evidence that the association is absent.

03 / How this research is kept honest

The question came
before the results.

The protocol was committed locally before downloading hospital outcomes. It preselected the discharge composite, hospital-wide readmission construct, hospital types, reliability minimum and exact date rule. It is an audit trail, not an externally registered study.

A minimum of 60 fully eligible Wisconsin hospitals was required before estimating an exploratory association. If too few remained with compatible dates, one expansion to Wisconsin, Illinois, Iowa, Michigan and Minnesota was allowed. The protocol explicitly prevents expanding to repair a dataset-level period mismatch. Expansion was not triggered.

What would still limit a matched analysis?

Hospitals differ in patient needs, staffing, resources and access to care after discharge. HCAHPS samples adults across payers; Medicare readmission reporting covers a different patient population. Hospital-level associations cannot show what happened to the same individual patients.

People can answer the survey after a readmission. Illness and the experience of returning to hospital may affect their ratings. Risk adjustment and a shared reporting window would not remove these problems.

What changed in HCAHPS in 2025?

Beginning with January 2025 discharges, CMS updated survey content and administration, including web-first modes, proxy responses and a longer response-collection period. Care Transition was removed. Discharge Information remained one of eight unchanged measures through transition; unchanged questions do not imply unchanged survey administration.

This pilot uses one HCAHPS snapshot spanning the transition. It does not pool releases or interchange Discharge Information, Care Transition and Care Coordination. The catalog file retrieved on October 29 was released August 13, 2026; a planned reporting timetable is not treated as proof that newer values were available.

Earlier research gives us reasons to ask—and to be careful.

01 / Randomized trial

A coordinated discharge package helped.

Jack and colleagues’ RED trial combined nurse discharge planning, education and medication work with a pharmacist follow-up call. The combined rate of emergency visits and readmissions fell; the readmissions-only interval included no effect. It cannot isolate the effect of written instructions.

Jack et al., 2009 · Full text reviewed ↗
02 / Timing matters

The rating can follow the return.

Siddiqui and colleagues found that only 15.8% of readmitted survey respondents answered before readmission. Ratings collected afterward can reflect that return, complicating the direction of the association.

Siddiqui et al., 2018 · Abstract reviewed ↗
03 / Different outcomes

A different clock changes the question.

Hydoub and colleagues studied 40,162 survey respondents across 20 hospitals, measuring admission after survey receipt. Discharge Information showed no association in the reported model. That outcome is different from 30 days after discharge.

Hydoub et al., 2023 · Full text reviewed ↗

This is a targeted evidence review, not a systematic review or a claim of novelty. The evidence table records access depth and overlapping studies, including a 2026 hospital-level study whose full methods could not be accessed.
